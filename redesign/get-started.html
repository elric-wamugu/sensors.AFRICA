From application to open data

How hosting a sensor works

Four steps, most of them ours. Your part takes an afternoon; the sensor does the rest around the clock.

1 Apply Fill in the short interest form — where you are, where the sensor could live, and whether you have Wi-Fi or would need a GSM (SIM-card) unit. We prioritise locations that fill gaps in the map: near schools, clinics, industry, busy roads. Takes about five minutes. Open the form.
2 Receive & assemble the kit Your kit arrives as a handful of off-the-shelf parts built to the open-source Luftdaten design — a dust sensor, a climate sensor, a small microcontroller and a weatherproof housing. Assembly is a no-solder job with step-by-step instructions, and in many cities a local workshop will build it with you.
3 Mount it & connect Fix the unit outside — a window ledge, balcony rail or wall, away from direct rain and exhaust vents — then connect it to your Wi-Fi, or use the GSM version where network coverage is patchy. Once it's online it needs almost nothing from you: just power and air.
4 Your data goes live Within minutes your readings start streaming to the open sensors.AFRICA platform — plotted on the live map, stored in the public archive, and free for journalists, researchers and your own neighbours to interrogate. Your street is now on the record. See it on the air quality map and in the open data archive.

The hardware

A lab's worth of measurement, a phone's worth of parts

The sensors.AFRICA air kit is a low-cost, modular device built on the open-source Luftdaten design that powers thousands of citizen sensors worldwide. Every component is off-the-shelf and replaceable — if a part fails, you swap it, not the whole unit.

That modularity is the point: the kit is cheap enough for a household, durable enough for a rooftop, and accurate enough for a newsroom. It measures the particulate matter that matters most to health — PM2.5 and PM10 — alongside the temperature and humidity needed to keep those readings honest.

Connectivity is flexible by design. Where Wi-Fi is reliable, the standard unit uses it. Where it isn't, a GSM variant reports over the mobile network instead — so coverage gaps in the internet don't become coverage gaps in the data.

What's in the kit

Standard air-quality build · Luftdaten design
SDS011 Dust sensor
Laser particulate sensor measuring PM2.5 and PM10 — the fine dust that penetrates deep into lungs and bloodstream.
DHT22 Climate sensor
Temperature and relative-humidity sensor — context that keeps particulate readings comparable across seasons and cities.
NodeMCU Microcontroller
The small ESP8266-based board that runs the open firmware, reads the sensors and ships the data to the platform.
Wi-Fi / SIM800L Connectivity
Built-in Wi-Fi as standard; a SIM800L GSM module where mobile networks are more dependable than broadband.
Housing & cabling Enclosure
Weatherproof piping enclosure, tubing and USB power — simple parts from any hardware store, built to survive sun and storm.
Design and firmware are open source — inspect, build or improve them at github.com/CodeForAfrica and luftdaten.info.
